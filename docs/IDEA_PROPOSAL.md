# SushrutAI: Idea Proposal

## 1. The problem

Many people in India don't know what to do when they feel unwell. Is this serious? What can I do at home? Which doctor, and how soon? Prescriptions are often handwritten, lab reports are full of unexplained abbreviations, and medicine boxes name brands rather than the generic salt, so cheaper options are missed. Several family members' medicines and vaccines get mixed up or forgotten. Online search adds unsafe guesses. The result is delayed care, wrongly taken medicines and money spent on expensive brands.

## 2. Who has it, where and how often

- **Patients** on their own phones, often in Hindi or another Indian language, some with low literacy. At home; several times a month, and daily when on medicines.
- **Family caregivers**, usually a grown child managing parents' and children's medicines and vaccines. Weekly, and in every illness; often phoning from work.
- **Elderly users** managing alone, with poor eyesight and no English. Daily for medicines.
- **Health workers** (ASHA and ANM) with many households a day, often with weak signal.

## 3. The voice-first product

The person speaks or types a symptom or question in their own language and hears the answer read aloud. Hands-free mode keeps listening. Each answer states an urgency level ("see a doctor today", "this week", "self-care"), and emergencies go straight to 108 or 112 before any model runs.

Beyond chat, it reads photographed prescriptions and lab reports line by line, finds cheaper generics using Jan Aushadhi prices, reminds people about doses and tracks vaccines against the government schedule.

Fixed principles: it gives health information, never a diagnosis or a dose, and "see a doctor" is always one tap away. It runs on the family's own computer; health data is encrypted per person and leaves the home only with a named, consented service. It currently speaks 13 languages, with Urdu laid out right to left.

## 4. Why voice comes first

- **No reading or typing needed.** People can ask in their own language, and read-aloud carries the answer back to older people and those with low literacy.
- **Symptoms are described, not typed.** People say "two din se bukhar aur badan dard"; voice keeps that natural wording.
- **Hands are busy.** Cooking, caring for a child or holding a patient is common, and a keyboard is useless then.
- **Shared phones.** A family member can help a parent through spoken replies without taking over the device.
- **Urgency is heard.** A spoken "call 108 now" is harder to miss than small text, especially for someone anxious.
- **It matches how people already talk.** Caregivers and health workers explain and reassure out loud; the app adds structured records behind that conversation.

## Open risks

- Medical content needs clinician and native-speaker review, especially for machine-translated languages.
- Small local models are slower and less accurate than large cloud models.
- Some voice models are licensed for non-commercial use and must be replaced before any commercial launch.
